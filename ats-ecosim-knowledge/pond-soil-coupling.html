Stabilizing pond and top-soil coupling

Scope: the local ATS–EcoSIM shared layer-0 implementation, with separate pond/litter and soil source terms. Source revisions: ATS 07f491d6, EcoSIM 435ab2b0, plus the existing uncommitted coupling changes. This report distinguishes measured behavior, experimental modifications, and proposed production work.

Current implementation: an exchange-timescale indicator reduces timesteps proactively while retaining separate mass and energy stores and the existing physical flux laws. The report includes the new implementation and the earlier hydraulic-resistance experiments.

Implemented: adaptive exchange indicator

The production code now includes the indicator approach. It selects smaller timesteps without adding the experimental hydraulic resistance or merging the two temperatures. The earlier resistance experiments below remain historical mechanism tests.

τapparent = [C₀,app / (1 + C₀,app/Cₛ,app)] / G C₀,safe = min(C₀,app, C₀,sensible) τsafe = [C₀,safe / (1 + C₀,safe/Cₛ,app)] / G χreference = Δtreference / τsafe

The sensible lower envelope is essential here: apparent capacity includes a latent-heat buffer that disappears when a nonlinear trial moves outside the freezing interval. This changes the timestep recommendation, not the heat capacity in the governing equations. Sensible capacity is assembled from the configured liquid/ice internal-energy derivatives and their energy weights, plus native dry litter heat capacity.

G uses top-cell thermal conductivity, face area, centroid-to-face distance, native layer-zero thermal resistance, and covered area exactly once. This is a conductive exchange-time estimate, not a complete eigenvalue estimate of the pressure–temperature Jacobian. Advective and phase-dependent flow responses still belong to the nonlinear solve.

At the original failing checkpoint: capacity contrast ≈ 19.54; τapparent ≈ 101,102 s; τsafe ≈ 263.24 s; χreference ≈ 13.68. The indicator therefore selects a 900-second cap before attempting the step.

All ranks evaluate their local surface columns and use the MPI-global minimum cap. The controller retains no new carried physical state; restart recomputes the indicator from the accepted fields. Retained water and native dry litter heat capacity participate in the capacity estimate, ready for future litter initialization.

3. Hydraulic resistance before an arbitrary flux cap

The failing input sets surface rel perm strategy = clobber and use surface rel perm = true. The boundary uses pond-based mobility while retaining soil intrinsic permeability. This motivates testing whether the frozen top-cell mobility should also enter the interface resistance; it does not prove the existing boundary discretization is incorrect.

q = (H₀ − Hₛ) / (Rpond/litter + Rtop-soil)

Here q is a volumetric flux per total surface area, H is hydraulic head, and hydraulic resistance has units of time. A production formulation must derive each resistance from the actual geometry, intrinsic permeability, viscosity, phase-dependent relative permeability, and covered area. Avoid counting top-cell resistance or pond coverage twice.

Do not cap q at conductivity alone. Conductivity must be multiplied by the hydraulic gradient. Do not cap infiltration using air-filled pores alone. This top cell is essentially filled by liquid plus ice, yet throughflow can occur when water leaves the cell simultaneously.

An admissible water balance must include downstream and lateral outflow, phase-volume changes, compressibility, and available liquid donor water. Enforce positivity through the nonlinear update, active constraints, or a conservative shared flux; independently clipping the two pool equations would break conservation.

4. Controlled experiment

Restart from the same accepted checkpoint checkpoint03376.h5. Keep forcing, source terms, mesh, solver tolerances, separate temperatures, and enthalpy definitions fixed. First reproduce failure with the restart control.

  1. Hourly baseline: unchanged pond-based mobility.
  2. Shorter timestep controls: subdivide the same physical hour. This tests temporal/nonlinear difficulty without changing the interface law.
  3. Soil-mobility control: use the existing interior-pressure boundary option. This deliberately omits the pond-freezing constraint and is a diagnostic, not a recommended production setting.
  4. Added soil resistance: retain pond freezing and coverage, and test a smooth additional resistance based on the top-soil relative permeability at the accepted start of each step.
r₀ = existing dimensionless pond mobility before the coverage multiplier
rₛ = top-soil k_relative(Sliquid), frozen during each nonlinear solve
rtest = r₀ rₛ / (rₛ + β r₀)
∂rtest/∂r₀ = rₛ² / (rₛ + β r₀)²

β = 0 recovers the baseline; β = 1 adds a full nominal soil resistance.
The existing coverage multiplier is applied once, after this transformation.

This is a mechanism test, not a calibrated geometric resistance law. It adds resistance to the existing boundary representation. The accepted-state soil coefficient is lagged; pond pressure and unfrozen-fraction derivatives are transformed analytically. A successful test would motivate a fully coupled implementation, not validate this approximation for production.

  • The instrumented hourly baseline matches all 240 original failing-hour ATS residual evaluations exactly, including temperatures and fluxes. Native hourly scratch arrays differ after restart because they are not checkpoint state; ATS reference sources match.
  • The added transformation's analytical derivative passes 48 finite-difference checks; maximum relative error is 2.86 × 10⁻⁶.
  • All successful one-hour tests pass the existing separate-pool budget validator. Saved checkpoint pairs in both successful fresh runs pass finite-value, source-mapping, and water/energy budget checks.
  • Detailed accepted-step checks cover day 140.5 through day 200: 5,712 consecutive pairs for 900 s and 1,428 pairs for β = 0.1. Logged inventories match the available accepted checkpoints exactly.
  • For the fresh β = 0.1 run, the largest individual-pool energy imbalance in that window is 7.31 × 10⁻⁵ W m⁻²; the largest water imbalance is 7.15 × 10⁻⁷ mol s⁻¹ m⁻² (0.0000464 mm h⁻¹). For the fresh 900-second run the corresponding maxima are 2.96 × 10⁻⁴ W m⁻² and 3.29 × 10⁻⁶ mol s⁻¹ m⁻².
  • Experiments run in Ubuntu 24.04 ARM64 Docker through isolated LD_PRELOAD libraries. Existing Amanzi, ATS and EcoSIM source diffs/status are unchanged; production executable and regression gold files were not replaced.
Interpretation: the results support interface mobility as one contributor to the nonlinear difficulty. They also show that small infiltration alone does not guarantee convergence. A single-temperature approximation was not needed for these successful cases, and has not been tested here.

Practical next step: use the 900-second case as a completed numerical control. Before promoting the hourly resistance prototype, derive the interface resistance from geometry, include the soil response in the coupled Jacobian/preconditioner, and test timestep and parameter sensitivity. β = 0.1 is an experimental weight, not a recommended material constant.

The two successful fresh runs differ in final top-soil temperature by 0.214 K and total column soil water by approximately 0.0193 kg m⁻². They also use different timesteps, so this comparison cannot isolate resistance error or establish accuracy.

5. Production solver strategy

Keep both temperatures

Use a local pressure–temperature block for pond and top soil. Include derivatives of phase-dependent mobility, hydraulic gradient, conductive heat exchange, and donor enthalpy. The current model already couples these processes; this proposal targets the accuracy of its Jacobian/preconditioner and globalization.

Consider total energy E₀ + Eₛ and a temperature difference as algebraic variables. Summing cancels internal heat exchange. An equivalent change of variables helps only when the solver and preconditioner use that structure.

Limit trial updates

Use line search or a trust region that respects available liquid water and follows the enthalpy–phase relation. Reduce timesteps around pond appearance and freeze–thaw transitions. Evaluate convergence and water/energy residuals together.

Any smoothing or regularization must be quantified through sensitivity and conservation checks, rather than selected only because it removes one failure.

6. Shared-temperature experiment

For a sufficiently fast equilibrating thin pond, impose T₀ = Tₛ = T and solve Ecombined(T,p₀,pₛ) = E₀(T,p₀) + Eₛ(T,pₛ). Retain separate water pools and their pressure relations; sum their energy equations and remove the internal exchange term. Include both latent-heat contributions and future dry litter heat capacity.

This removes one stiff temperature-difference mode, but also removes the physical pond–soil thermal gradient. Judge the approximation using equilibration time τ ≈ [C₀Cₛ/(C₀+Cₛ)]/G, phase state, interface resistance, and timestep. Near melting, use enthalpy or tangent capacities rather than sensible capacity alone. Do not merely replace pond heat capacity with soil heat capacity while keeping two independent energy balances.

7. Acceptance and evidence

  • The identical failing hour converges with the original tolerances; record nonlinear iterations, rejected trials, and temperature/flux ranges.
  • Both water and energy budgets close, including EcoSIM sources, internal exchange, and column boundaries.
  • Then extend across subsequent days and the 200-day case. Passing one restarted hour is insufficient.
  • Check frozen, thawed, dry, partially ponded, snow-covered, and future litter cases; retain separate-source ownership.
